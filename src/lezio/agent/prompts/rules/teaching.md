TEACHING GUIDELINES
How to make a lesson people learn well from, drawn from research on multimedia learning and educational
video. They are defaults, not rules: use judgment for each topic and audience. The user's instructions
override them; the depth decides how much is added to the material (questions, recaps and visuals add no
new content; extra examples and analogies belong to the deeper levels or come from the material itself).
Guidance helps beginners and gets in the way of experts: adapt it to the level the material is written for.

LESSON STRUCTURE
- Open with something that makes the viewer want the answer: an on-topic puzzle, a surprising fact, a
  "what would you predict?" question - then a short roadmap of the parts. Never an off-topic hook.
- Why before how: show the need or the problem first, then a concrete example, then the general rule and
  its notation (concrete -> schematic -> abstract, each step visibly linked to the previous one).
- Name and show the parts and key terms before explaining how they work together; with tightly
  interconnected material, present the elements one at a time, then their interactions.
- One idea per segment, with a clear boundary: a spoken signpost ("now the second step"), a new title or
  scene, a short hold on the key visual at the end.
- Give the lesson an arc: the opening question comes back and is answered at the end.
- Close with a recap that makes the viewer retrieve: ask, pause, then show the answer - not a list that
  only repeats what was said.

CHOOSING HOW TO SHOW EACH THING
- Let the voice explain and the screen show. On screen: the visual, key terms, labels, formulas, numbers,
  the problem statement - not the sentences being spoken.
- Diagram: for structure, spatial relations, causal chains, many relations that must be seen together.
  Label the parts on the diagram itself; cut it down to what the narration needs.
- Animation: only when change is what must be learned - a process, a motion, a transformation, timing,
  a procedure. Keep it schematic and slow, cue what changes, split it into short steps, and leave the
  final state on screen. Structure, discrete steps and comparisons are better as a sequence of still
  states or one diagram with arrows. No motion without meaning (fly-ins, spins, bouncing).
- Build up instead of showing a finished complex figure: parts first, then their relations, each element
  appearing when the voice names it; earlier steps stay visible. Writing or drawing it as if by hand is a
  way to build up (the benefit is the synchronized build-up, not the handwriting look).
- Worked examples and derivations: one transformation per step, the changed term highlighted, earlier
  lines kept on screen, steps grouped under short subgoal labels, and the voice saying why each step is
  allowed. After a full example, a similar problem: "pause and try", a pause, then the solution.
- Analogy: only with a source the viewer knows; show source and target side by side, aligned, with
  explicit links between corresponding parts; say where the analogy stops working.
- Comparison: show the cases at the same time, side by side, ask what they share or how they differ,
  then state the principle. For a concept, pair examples with non-examples that differ only in the
  critical feature.
- Misconception: when the material touches a common wrong idea, state it, let the viewer commit to a
  prediction, show why it fails, then give the right model - a clear explanation alone leaves it intact.
- Data: a table for exact values, a line chart for trends, bars for discrete comparisons; label directly,
  no pies or 3D for precise comparisons.
- Original pages and figures: show them when they are what is studied (real data, a photo, a document,
  a figure the learner will meet again in their material). Redraw a schematic when the original is
  cluttered, must be built up or animated, or has labels far from their parts; when both appear, link
  them with the same layout and colors.
- Text only is fine when the content truly has no useful visual: then show the few key words, large.

THE SCREEN WHILE THE VOICE TALKS
- What the voice names is what lights up, at that moment: one highlight at a time (color, frame, arrow,
  dimming the rest). Signal in words too: "three steps", "first", "second".
- Keep on screen what the viewer must hold in mind while listening (formula, definition, problem, the
  list of steps) for as long as it is used: speech disappears, the screen can stay.
- Labels of a few words, placed on the part they name; never a legend far away.
- After a reveal leave a moment to look before the next new element; never a new visual and a dense new
  sentence at the same instant.
- One color per concept for the whole lesson, the same in text, formulas and figures (the plan's visual
  language); color always means something.
- Nothing decorative: no stock pictures, ornaments, off-topic anecdotes or effects - least of all
  anything that stays on screen next to the content.

NARRATION AND PACING
- Conversational - talk to the viewer ("you", "we") -, warm, with real enthusiasm for what matters; no
  filler. Speech that is too long or dense over a busy screen overloads: keep pieces short.
- Slow down at key claims and new symbols (a pause after them), move briskly through transitions.
- Prompt the viewer to think: predict before a reveal, "why do you think...", a question at the end of a
  segment with a pause of three to five seconds before the answer. A smooth "that was clear" feeling is
  not the goal; understanding is.
